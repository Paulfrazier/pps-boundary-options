PPS’s reasons for closing schools, from its own documents

Why close schools at all?

PPS’s reasons are spread across a 63-page memo, ten closure reports and months of Board slides. Here they are in one place, from the district’s own memo, closure reports and Board slides, so you can judge the trade for yourself.

A note from me: I’m not taking a stance for or against closing schools. I’m trying to lay out the math as I see it, using PPS’s own numbers, so you can weigh it yourself.

The short version

  1. Portland has 6,000 fewer students than in 2018, and the drop isn’t over. About 30% fewer babies were born than a decade earlier. Elementary schools took most of the hit.
  2. PPS kept every building. So 33 of 45 elementary schools now have fewer than 360 kids, PPS’s minimum for a full program.
  3. Small schools stretch thin. Two grades in one class, art and music teachers shared between buildings. And every building needs a principal, an office and a custodian whether it holds 200 kids or 500.
  4. PPS must cut about $65 million either way. Closing 10 buildings means about $10 million of those cuts come from duplicate principals, offices and custodians instead of from inside schools.

What it means for your child

If nothing changes

By 2031, only 18% of elementary schools would reach PPS’s 360-student minimum. Small schools would keep stretching: more blended grades (already 17% of K-5 teaching positions), shared art and music teachers, principals pulled onto lunch and recess duty. PPS still has to cut about 223 positions, and none could come from closing duplicate buildings.

If your school is closing

Your child moves in fall 2027 or 2028 to a nearby school. Most receiving schools would be 32–70% full with no change, and 64–107% full with the move. PPS reserves 15–20 positions for the schools taking students in. PPS measured every elementary school against the same 360-student yardstick. Find your school below.

PPS figures: memo, p. 1 (18%), memo, p. 5 and the Sept 8, 2026 Board committee deck, p. 9 (blended grades, shared teachers, principal “on duty 2-3 hours a day”), each closure report’s section 3a (principals on lunch and recess duty), Board Q&A tracker, p. 35 (125 of 717 K-5 teaching positions went to blended classrooms), memo, pp. 50–51 (223 positions; with no changes “the district captures 0 FTE in structural savings”), memo, p. 2 (15–20 positions reserved for receiving schools). 3. What “stretched thin” means for your child

PPS has kept small schools going with one-time COVID money, reserves, bigger classes, fewer support staff, and about 40 positions moved from high schools to prop up K-8s and immersion programs. PPS showed its Board what that looks like, and what bigger schools could offer instead:

Three small schools, 200–350 kids each

  • Two grades in one class
  • Art and music teachers split across buildings
  • Library open part of the week
  • Less coaching and extra help for kids who are behind
  • Principal on duty 2–3 hours a day
  • Few electives for 6th–8th graders

Two schools, 360–500 kids each

  • One grade per class
  • A full arts program in the building
  • Coaching and extra help restored
  • Principal free to lead teaching
  • Library open more of the week

Already, 17% of the K-5 teaching positions PPS handed out this year went to blended classrooms.

Sources and notes

The two pictures: PPS, Sept 8, 2026 Board committee, pp. 7, 9–10 (original). How PPS has coped: memo, p. 5. Blended classrooms: 125 of 717 K-5 positions, Board Q&A tracker, p. 35. In fairness to small schools: PPS says every K-5 student still gets two days of arts, two of PE and one of library each week (same tracker, p. 31).

5. The money: the cuts come either way

PPS has a $65 million budget gap for 2027-28 and plans to cut about 223 positions to close it. Closing schools doesn’t fix that. It decides where $10.2 million a year of those cuts land: on duplicate building overhead (principals, office staff, custodians, utilities), or on something else.

8. What PPS still owes families

PPS’s accounting still has gaps worth asking about:

  1. Busing. Do the new bus routes (about $2.7 million) and immersion bus changes (about $3 million) come out of the $10.2 million in savings?
  2. What’s left for schools. PPS says $7.1 million is left to put back into schools. Subtracting moving costs its own total leaves out, and its 5% contingency, it’s closer to $5.7 million.
  3. Building repairs in dollars. PPS cites repairs (Beach’s roof, Sabin’s plumbing) as reasons but never prices them. The only figures are 2024 seismic estimates and small upgrade lines for receiving schools.
  4. Boundary changes alone. PPS says redrawing lines couldn’t balance enrollment, but hasn’t shown the numbers.
Sources and notes

Bus routes: memo, p. 55. $7.1 million and its moving costs: memo, p. 50 (our arithmetic subtracts the $1.08 million in program-move and grade-change costs and the 5% contingency). Boundary changes: memo, p. 14.
