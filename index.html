PPS boundary options: a parent’s look at what else is possible

These are best effort boundaries. NOT REAL PLANS, yet.

Independent analysis by a PPS parent, not an official Portland Public Schools product. It may contain errors; check PPS’s rightsizing scenarios and the October 6, 2026 board materials. Built with AI, directed by a PPS parent (how).

PPS has shared two scenarios, PPS A and PPS B. This site models what other options could look like, with different boundaries and the trade-offs each one makes. The goal: to encourage PPS to release more of its data and present more options. Keeping PPS A’s closures but redrawing the lines puts more kids within walking distance and keeps more schools at their size target. Under the best example we modeled, 67.6% of elementary kids live within a 1-mile walk of their school, against 62.7% under PPS A (72.2% with no closures). On our model, all 35 neighborhood elementary schools stay at their size target every year to 2031-32, many with thin margins (see the costs below). PPS A keeps 21 at target on our model, and 18 on PPS’s own projections.

What it costs: smaller middle schools, cleaner feeder paths and more moves. The best example we modeled balances middle schools with a minimum of 460, below PPS’s 500 goal: four dip below 500 in their low year, so 9 of 13 meet PPS’s 500 every year to 2031-32 (5 under PPS A). In return, only 3 elementary schools split between two middle schools (1 under PPS A) and no middle school is over capacity on our model. 77% of a middle-school class goes on to the same high school (90% under PPS A), slightly fewer middle schoolers live within a mile (28.9% vs 30.3%), and about 7,200 elementary and middle schoolers change schools compared with PPS Status Quo, against about 4,900 under PPS A. A sibling version, Boundary changes, same closures, uses the same elementary lines and holds middle schools to PPS’s 500: 12 of 13 meet it every year, but 13 elementary schools split between middle schools.

Trade-offs

No plan wins every row: that is the trade-off.

Every number by level, with bus lines and racial mix: Options, side by side.

What the trade-offs show

  • PPS’s closures can work with other lines. Starting from PPS A’s closures, every example plan keeps at least as many schools at target as PPS A every year to 2031-32, on our model.
  • Shorter walks move more kids. Whether that’s worth it is the district’s call.
  • Walking and feeders pull against each other. Walkable middle-school lines split more feeders. Redrawing every level together didn’t fix it: the Ida B. Wells and Grant areas have too few middle schoolers for their buildings, under PPS A too.
  • Sizing to one year is fragile. Enrollment keeps falling after 2031-32; see Durability.

Our goals

  1. Show more options. Two scenarios are two points in a wide space. We lay out more, side by side, so the trade-offs are visible.
  2. Ask the district for more options. PPS has better data than we do. Our numbers are model estimates, built from Census blocks rather than PPS’s student records, so they may not hold up on PPS’s real data. We’d like PPS to test options like these on its own data and present more than two, including lines that keep kids closer to school and schools at target every year to 2031-32.
  3. Publish the data. Our data, code and method are open. We’d like PPS to share the data behind its scenarios too: boundary files in a mapping format (not just PDFs), student counts on a privacy-safe grid, the transfer and program-follow assumptions behind each scenario, and transportation costs.

Why the modeling stops here. We could keep pushing the models: more runs, finer lines, new trade-offs to test. But past this point, more modeling adds precision the inputs can’t support. Our student counts are estimates built from 2020 Census blocks and calibrated to 2025-26 enrollment, and PPS hasn’t published the scenario-level data behind A and B: student counts on a privacy-safe grid, the transfer and program-follow assumptions under each scenario, and cost estimates. With the district’s data, the same tools could test plans far more precisely. Without it, further tuning would mostly fit our own estimates.

The aim: a community that can talk through the trade-offs with the same numbers in front of everyone.

Shorter walks and more schools at target, at a cost

Why redrawn lines do better

  • PPS A’s line changes are targeted. It folds each closing school into one or two nearby schools and makes 38 targeted boundary changes (PPS scenario memo, Oct 5, 2026), but it doesn’t redraw lines around every school, so some end up too big and others too small: 20 elementary and middle schools out of range in 2031-32 on our model. PPS’s own projections show the same, such as Clark at 272 (343 with no closures) and Sunnyside, as a K-5, at 235 (the floor is 360), and George at 636 in a 623-seat building (PPS Family Guides, p. 4; capacity from PPS’s 2025-26 school profiles, second-hand).
  • PPS A adds a little to long walks; redrawing removes a lot. With today’s schools and no closures (PPS Status Quo), 1,861 elementary kids are already assigned to a farther school, past a closer open one. PPS A raises that by 109, to 1,970. The best example we modeled cuts it to 932: 929 fewer than with no closures. Sending kids to the closer school also evens out sizes.
  • Size comes first. Every example plan must keep at least as many schools at target as PPS A, every year to 2031-32; within that, it finds the shortest walks.

The costs

  • Messier feeder paths. More elementary schools split between two middle schools, and fewer middle-school classes stay together into high school (numbers above).
  • More kids change schools: about 7,100 elementary and middle schoolers under the best example we modeled, against about 4,900 under PPS A (both compared with PPS Status Quo).
  • Thin size margins. Many elementary schools sit just above the 360-kid floor: in 2031-32, 19 to 22 of 35 are within 20 kids of it across the example plans (22 under the best example we modeled), against 2 under PPS A. That works if PSU’s forecast holds, as PPS assumes; if enrollment falls faster, those schools dip below 360 first.

A what-if, not a proposal

What if focus options were sized too?

We tested what happens to school sizes and walks if Winterhaven and da Vinci closed and their students returned to neighborhood schools. This is a sensitivity test, not a recommendation to close either school.

It rests on a second-hand count of where focus-option students live (source of the per-program numbers not stated) and assumes every returning student stays in PPS.
