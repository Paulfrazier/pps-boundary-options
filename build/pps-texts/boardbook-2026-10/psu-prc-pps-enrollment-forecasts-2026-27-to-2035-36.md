EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This report presents the methodology and findings of a demographic study conducted by the Portland
State University Population Research Center (PRC) on behalf of Portland Public Schools (PPS). It
includes an analysis of demographic, housing, and enrollment trends that affect PPS, as well as detailed
annual enrollment forecasts for the district and its individual schools, categorized by grade as of October
1 for each academic year.
1.1
Demographic Trends
Between 2000 and 2010, the population of the PPS area grew at an annualized rate of 0.8%, increasing
from 425,932 to 459,982 residents. Growth accelerated in the following decade; between the 2010 and
2020 decennial censuses, the population grew by 1.2% annually, reaching 519,048 residents. The 2021
American Community Survey (ACS) estimated a subsequent one-year population decrease of 2.1% (to
508,401), likely due to disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. However, between 2021 and
2023, the ACS indicates that the PPS area returned to growth at an annualized rate of 0.6%, reaching
514,653 before stabilizing at 513,647 in 2024. The percentage of Portland’s total population residing
within the PPS boundary remains at approximately 81%.
The PPS area is younger on average than the rest of the state, featuring a significantly higher pro-
portion of residents aged 20 to 39 years (36%, compared to 27% statewide) and a lower female median
age (38.2, compared to 41.9 statewide). Despite its younger demographic, Portland’s birth rate has de-
clined significantly over the past 20 years and sits well below the state average. Approximately 2.7% of
women aged 15 to 50 reported having given birth in the past year, compared to 4.4% statewide. Notably,
the city’s total fertility rate – an approximation of the expected lifetime births per woman – has dropped
from 1.89 in 2010 to 0.84 in 2024. This persistent downward trend exerts downward pressure on PPS
enrollment, which can only be offset by an increase in the in-migration of children or a decline in the
share of children in private or alternative schooling options.
The U.S. Census Bureau counted 253,369 housing units in the PPS zone in 2020, up from 219,365 in
2010 (an annual growth rate of 1.4%). According to the Regional Land Information System, by January
1, 2026, the total number of housing units had increased further to 270,599, maintaining a steady an-
nualized growth rate of approximately 1.3%. Multifamily structures have accounted for approximately
84% of new housing units since 2010 and tend to consist of smaller rental units with fewer children.
Consequently, the average household size has trended downward. In 2024, the average household size
in the PPS area was 2.02 (2.34 for owner-occupied housing and 1.70 for renter-occupied units). The

1.2
Enrollment Trends
In fall 2025, Portland Public Schools (PPS) enrolled 42,622 students in grades K–12, a decrease of 753
students from fall 2024 and sitting just over 6,000 students below the pre-pandemic baseline of 2019–
20. Post-pandemic trajectories have diverged among individual schools within the PPS system; some
have returned to pre-pandemic enrollment patterns, while others continue to experience persistently low
enrollment in their neighborhoods. The overall resident capture rate in the district has increased from
74.4% in fall 2019 to an estimated 76.6% in 2025. However, this recent recovery is still 5.6 percentage
points lower than the district’s previous high of 82.2% in 2015.
Enrollment declined by 7.5% during the two-year period from fall 2019 to fall 2021. This contrac-
tion was primarily attributable to household shifts during the COVID-19 pandemic, either by moving
outside the district boundaries or opting for alternative educational options, including private schools,
online charter schools, or homeschooling.
The greatest declines during the COVID-19 pandemic were observed in the elementary grades.
Specifically, cohorts entering kindergarten (K) were much smaller in 2020–21 and 2021–22, as the share
of the population aged 5 enrolled in a PPS school (“K capture rate”) decreased from approximately 73%
in 2019 to 68% in 2020 and 70% in 2021. In 2022, the capture rate for the K cohort returned to 73%
and has fluctuated from 77% in 2023 to an estimated 74% in 2025.
District-wide middle school enrollment experienced mostly steady growth between 2010 and 2019.
The pandemic coincided with a reversal of the trend. In the fall of 2021, there was a loss of nearly 700
students in 6th–8th grade when schools returned to in-person learning with masking. The subsequent
two years saw average losses of 250 students per year. These losses were only partially due to the
pandemic: elementary enrollment declines, driven by low birth rates and a declining K capture rate,
began to appear as early as 2013 and were felt when those entering cohorts reached sixth grade in
the fall of 2019. By fall of 2024 and 2025, the middle school grades lost only 18 and 53 students,
respectively, which may reflect students who left during the pandemic returning to the district at the
middle school entry point.
High school enrollment increased throughout the pandemic; however, it has now begun to decline.
Between fall of 2020 and fall 2021, enrollment in 9th–12th grade increased by 252 students, followed by
an additional 53 students between 2021 and 2022. This resulted in a total enrollment of 14,389 students
in the fall of 2022, the highest level since 2005–2006. High school enrollment numbers remained
relatively stable for the following two years. However, between the fall of 2024 and the fall of 2025,

1.3
Summary of Enrollment Forecast Results
Enrollment is projected to continue to decline from the 2025 fall level to 36,763 in 2035–36. Subse-
quently, enrollment is projected to level off, declining by less than 1% per year over the following 5
years, as historically small entry classes from the COVID period age out and higher capture rates in
earlier grades yield larger incoming classes.
The kindergarten and first-grade capture rates are predictive of an age cohort’s future attachment to
PPS. To maintain enrollment, PPS will need more school-age children in the district or a higher share
of the age-eligible population to choose to enroll in a PPS classroom at these early grade levels.
In the fall of 2020, amid the COVID-19 pandemic, the resident kindergarten capture rate hit a 15-
year low of 68%; however, it has since recovered, reaching approximately 74% in the fall of 2025. The
K capture rate is expected to continue to increase to 79% by the fall of 2028.
Another factor putting downward pressure on enrollments is that the underlying population aged 5,
which forms the pool eligible for kindergarten enrollment, has also been declining. This is the result of
declining birth rates since 2010 and is not expected to reverse within the enrollment forecast horizon.
In addition, more children under the age of 5 tend to migrate out of the PPS district with their families
than migrate in. As a result, all future growth in kindergarten and first grade enrollment is expected to
come from capturing a larger share of the age-eligible population entering PPS.
In total, PPS kindergartens are expected to enroll 2,969 students in the 2026–27 school year, an
increase of 50 students from the fall 2025 enrollment. Overall, district-wide enrollment for grades K–5
is expected to decline from 18,695 to 18,427 students. The decline is projected to continue until the
2037–38 school year, when enrollment will begin a slight recovery from its low of 16,435.
District-wide enrollment for grades 6–8 will see declines in fall 2026, from 9,753 to 9,590 (-1.7%).
As smaller elementary cohorts progress through the middle school grades, enrollment will continue to
decline to a low of 7,624 students in 2040–41.
High schools were resilient during the pandemic, even as elementary and middle schools saw large
declines. District-wide enrollment for grades 9–12 is projected to decline in fall 2026, from 14,174 to
13,904 students (-1.9%). Eventually, the impact of smaller classes in elementary and middle schools
will be felt as declining high school cohort sizes. Although some years may see minor fluctuations, the
overall trend is towards declining high school enrollment, falling to 11,462 in the 2040–41 school year.

POPULATION AND HOUSING TRENDS
From 2000 to 2010, the population within the PPS boundary grew steadily, increasing by 34,050 resi-
dents from 425,932 to 459,982. This growth trend accelerated in the following decade, with a gain of
59,066 from 2010 to 2020. In 2020, the district population reached 519,048. However, by 2024, the
district population is estimated to have decreased to 513,647.
Table 2.1 shows these changes alongside city and statewide trends. Between 2000 and 2010, the
district’s growth rate was 8%, lower than that of both the City of Portland (10.3%) and the state of
Oregon (12%). Conversely, between 2010 and 2020, the District’s growth rate accelerated to 12.8%,
outpacing both the city and state. Following the COVID-19 pandemic, the total population of the PPS
district fell by approximately 1%, less than the 2.4% in the city of Portland but more than the 0.8%
increase in the state of Oregon as a whole. As a result, because the city’s population declined faster
than the school district’s, the proportion of Portland’s overall city population residing within the PPS
boundary steadily expanded from 79% in 2010 to 81% in 2024. Figure 2.1 illustrates the boundaries
and locations of the PPS schools.

2.2
Births
Despite the growing number of young adults in the District, the average number of births per woman
under age 30 declined significantly. Figure 2.3 highlights this transformation using age-specific fertility
rates (ASFRs) for women across age groups. By 2020, ASFRs for women in the PPS area under 25
had dropped by approximately 72% from their 1990 levels, while the rates for those aged 25 to 29 had
dropped by nearly 60% from their 1990 levels. The number of births to women under age 25 within the
PPS area fell from 1,768 in 2000 to 860 in 2010, and further down to 294 by 2020.
The total fertility rate (TFR), an indicator of the average number of children a woman is expected
to have during her childbearing years, has also seen a notable decrease. In 2000, the TFR was 1.67; by
2010, it had dropped to 1.36, and by 2024, it was 0.77. The falling fertility rates among women under 30
were partially offset by increases among women over 30, alongside overall population growth, thereby
slowing the decline in the number of births within PPS. Despite the increase in the number of women
in prime childbearing ages, the annual number of births has been decreasing since 2010, with a 25.7%
reduction between 2010 and 2020 and an additional 9.4% by 2024, as illustrated in Figure 2.4.
In addition, the median age of women giving birth has increased significantly. In 2010, the median
ages were 26 and 24 years in PPS and in the state as a whole, respectively. By 2020, this age rose to 30
years for PPS residents, compared to 27 years in the state, where the median age of women giving birth
has remained at that level through 2025, as shown in Figure 2.5.
Table 2.3 shows the birth trends in HSCLs in the last 15 years, divided into three 5-year periods. All
HSCLs experienced declines in births between the first two periods, with only Lincoln’s HSCL limited
to single-digit declines. Similarly, all clusters saw double-digit declines in the most recent two periods
with the exception of Ida B. Wells-Barnett and Lincoln HSCLs, which both held to a 9% decline.

Housing Trends
Figure 2.6 illustrates the annual totals of new single-family and multi-family housing units permitted
in the district from 2000 to 2025. The general trend across PPS has been greater housing unit density,

2.4
Affordable Housing
Housing affordability is defined by the price of housing relative to income. Affordable housing is often
defined as housing that costs no more than 30% of a household’s income, providing a margin for saving
or spending in other areas. PPS’s housing values are far higher than the rest of the state (median home
value in Portland was 634,200 in 2024, well above the state average of 497,500 and the national average
of 360,600). Rental prices are closer to the statewide average (the average renter in PPS paid $1,659 in
2023 compared to $1,597 statewide). However, Portland housing units are on average much smaller–
studio and 1-bedroom units comprise nearly 32% of Portland housing, compared to 14% statewide.
Housing affordability has been a growing problem. The share of renter households paying more
than 30% of their income on rent has continued to increase from 48.5% in 2019 to 51% in 2024. The
production of affordable housing is a priority for state and local governments, and affordable housing
and home ownership are associated with better health and educational outcomes for children. Afford-
able housing is also important for school planning because affordable units are more likely to house
families with children than market-rate apartments. Table 2.6 demonstrates this by comparing the aver-
age number of children per household for a variety of housing types. Single-family housing and large
apartments with 3 or more bedrooms–especially those in affordable housing developments–are much
more likely than other types of multi-family housing to have children.

The capture rate is the ratio of enrollment in District schools to the school-age population living within

ment includes some students who are not included in the district’s school-age population, specifically
transfer students, intra- and inter-district transfers, and students over age 18.

In Oregon, families may choose to homeschool their child at any time and for any duration. Accord-
ing to the laws 1, when families choose to homeschool their child, they are required to file a notification

the number of new homeschool registrations in all Multnomah County districts increased from 446 to
1659, almost quadrupling in one year, followed by an additional 835 registrations in the 2021-22 school
year. In November 2023, the Multnomah ESD reported PPS had 1585 registered homeschool students.
Registrations have declined since that time, but remained elevated in historical terms. As of November
2025, there were approximately 787 PPS residents registered to homeschool, which is approximately
1.5% of the estimated age-eligible population in the district. 2
3.2
Enrollment Trends by Place of Residence
The overall population of students residing in an attendance area and enrolled in any PPS school is
typically more stable than the enrollment at the neighborhood school serving the attendance area. En-
rollment at individual schools may change due to program or boundary changes, school openings or
closures, school choice, the number of transfer slots, or other factors unrelated to underlying demo-
graphic trends. When student points are matched by address in a geographic information system, the
number of PPS students (including charter schools) by grade level can be tabulated for any geographic
area. Creating a time series of resident PPS students by grade level and current attendance area facil-
itates historical enrollment analysis, even if school boundaries have changed, allowing us to identify
shifts in the share of area students who enroll in their neighborhood school, attend other PPS schools,
or participate in PPS programs.
Table 3.3 shows enrollment trends by grade levels across HSCLs for years 2020 through 2025, along
with the numeric and percentage changes over the five years. For K-5 enrollment, the district saw a sig-
nificant overall decline of 14.4% across all clusters except Other Programming. The most significant
decreases are seen in the clusters of Cleveland (-20.4%), Franklin (-19.2%), and Jefferson/McDaniel
and Grant (each -16.7%). Other Programming saw an increase of 9%, while the Lincoln cluster saw
the smallest decline at -8%. The trend of 6-8 enrollment over five years is similar to K-5, with an
overall district decrease of 11.5%, except for Jefferson/McDaniel and Jefferson/Roosevelt, which show
increases of 2.3% and 0.4%, respectively. The most dramatic drop is observed in the Roosevelt cluster
(-22.1%). Contrary to the trends in lower grades, 9-12 high school enrollment appears to be increasing
across most clusters, with an overall district increase of 0.6%. Notable increases are seen in Jeffer-
son/McDaniel (15.8%) and Grant (13.6%), while larger decreases were seen in Other Programming
(-10.7%) and Franklin (-8%).

ENROLLMENT FORECASTS
The enrollment forecasts comprise several components, beginning with a demographic forecast of the
district’s total resident population and the expected share of that population that enrolls in a PPS school
at the kindergarten entry point. The growth or attrition of currently enrolled cohorts is subsequently
determined by modeling and projecting the ratio of students who advance to the next grade in school.
The overall methodology utilizes a bottom-up, school-level forecast controlled to a top-down district
total. This ensures consistency across individual school results and the broader district, while leveraging
the more stable, less volatile historical trends of the district-wide model.
4.1
Forecast Process
The first step in the enrollment forecast is to produce historical annual estimates and a forecast of the
PPS area’s resident population. The historical estimates provide denominators for calculating fertility,
mortality, and migration rates in the district. These rates are, in turn, projected by age and sex to
estimate the future eligible population for each grade level of enrollment at PPS.
The second step is to model and project district-wide kindergarten capture rates and subsequent
grade progression ratios (GPR). The ratio of resident students enrolled in PPS to the district-resident
eligible population (capture rate) is projected and applied to the demographic forecast to estimate the
number of incoming kindergarten students. For students in all subsequent grades, a continuation rate or
GPR is used to project enrollment as a function of the prior year’s enrollment in the preceding grade (for
example, the size of an incoming kindergarten class may be 75% of the eligible resident 5-year-olds,
while the upcoming 1st-grade class may be 98% of the prior year’s kindergarten class).
Each individual school has an independent forecast as well. For most schools, the model calcu-
lates new kindergarten intake as a share of the total district-wide kindergarten enrollment. However,
for specific schools where district-share mapping is less accurate, kindergarten enrollment is projected
independently using localized historical trends. Each school’s GPRs are applied to project all subse-
quent grades. In cases where schools have experienced attendance zone changes or program moves,
data splits are created such that the zones or programs can be characterized as they were in the past,
or as they will be in the future. Program moves, attendance boundary changes, facility openings and
closures, and other systemic adjustments - including implementation of Southeast Enrollment and Pro-
gram Balancing - are incorporated into the enrollment forecast based on data provided by PPS on their
timing, geographic scope, and affected populations.

In a third step, additional adjustments are made for the number of students expected to impact PPS
enrollment from planned affordable housing developments featuring units with two or more bedrooms.
While the future pace of market-generated housing is assumed to be captured within recent baseline
trends for each neighborhood and school, affordable developments are not entirely market-driven. Be-
cause larger affordable units have higher-than-average numbers of children per unit, these developments
are treated as an exogenous source of students for the specific schools serving those neighborhoods.
These adjustments serve to distribute the projected student population to the specific schools serving
those developments, ensuring localized housing impacts are captured within the individual school fore-
casts.
The final step is an adjustment and reconciliation phase that addresses cases where capture rates
or GPRs should be adjusted to account for outliers or unusual circumstances, and to ensure internal
consistency. Discrepancies between the individual school and district-wide forecasts by grade are re-
solved by adjusting individual school metrics. Through this process, individual school forecasts are
mathematically consistent with the higher-level district control totals by adjusting school-level capture
rates at kindergarten and GPRs for the remaining grades.
4.2
Forecast Data and Methodology
The historical resident population of PPS is estimated from the decennial census by summing the counts
for individual census blocks that comprise the district, broken down by age and sex. Between censuses,
tract-level interpolations are used, weighted based on the average share of the population in each tract
that is within the district, determined by which blocks in each tract are inside or outside the district.
Birth and death rates within the district are estimated from vital statistics records of actual births and
deaths that occurred within the district boundaries, divided by the historical population estimates. They
are projected by a variant of the Lee-Carter method, based on an average age pattern for birth or death
rates, and one or more sets of deviations that, when combined, best explain the historical deviations
from the average pattern, along with accompanying weights that explain how each set of deviations
has increased or decreased in importance over time. Migration is forecasted by an auto-regressive time
series model that assumes a gradual return to the long-run pre-COVID trend (Figure 4.1).
The resident population forecast is generated by starting with the actual age structure from the
2020 Census and projecting it forward annually by adding or subtracting expected births, deaths, and
migrants by age and sex to reflect expected changes each year.
Student enrollment data as of October 1 of each school year, by grade, school attended, and school
zone of residence, was provided by PPS. Data from 2017 through 2025 was used in the current forecasts.
The kindergarten capture rate is estimated from enrollment data using resident population data for the
corresponding kindergarten-eligible age cohort, while grade-progression ratios are calculated directly
from historical enrollment data. Both the capture rate and GPRs at many schools were exceptionally low
during the COVID-19 pandemic; however, they have since mostly recovered to pre-pandemic levels.
The kindergarten capture rate is projected based on the assumption of gradual convergence toward
the pre-COVID 10-year average, while the district-wide GPRs use the prior 3-year averages, and the
individual schools generally rely on longer-run averages with the COVID years removed (Figures 4.2

4.3.1
District-wide Enrollment Forecasts
The K-12 enrollment of 41,921 students in fall 2026 is 701 students (1.64%) lower than the fall 2025
total, and just over 6,700 students below the fall 2019 pre-pandemic level. Enrollment is projected to
continue to decline, at a decelerating rate, until reaching a low point of 35,773 in 2040-41 (Figure 4.4).
Kindergarten and first-grade capture rates are predictive of an age cohort’s future attachment to PPS.
Currently, kindergarten and first-grade capture rates are recovering from their pandemic lows, reaching
approximately 74% for kindergarten and 80% for first-grade populations. Despite their recovery, they
still remain below their historical highs of 86% for kindergarten and 90% for first grade in the fall of
2013. The size of the entering kindergarten cohorts has been declining since its peak in 2012, while the
underlying population aged 5, which forms the pool eligible for kindergarten enrollment, was in steady
decline from 2019 to 2024, with a slight increase in 2025. This pressure on enrollment stems from birth
rates that have declined in most years since 2010 and are expected to remain low over the enrollment
forecast horizon. In the projections, the kindergarten capture rate is expected to rise to 79% by 2028.
The enrollment projections reflect an optimistic near- and medium-term outlook on net migration
to Portland, which was negative in 2020 and 2021. Migration tends to bring young residents into the
district; however, the average household moving out of the district is more likely to include children.
If these trends continue, future growth in kindergarten/first enrollment must come from a larger share
of the age-eligible population entering PPS, or from transfer students, because migration is unlikely to
contribute any net school-age children. Furthermore, the enrollment projections reflect a higher GPR
for the first grade, consistent with the district’s historical trend of capturing additional students as the
cohort transitions from kindergarten.

Enrollment Forecasts by Grade
PPS kindergarten classrooms are expected to enroll 2,969 students in the 2026–27 school year, an in-
crease of 50 students from fall 2025 (1.7%). The growth of the kindergarten cohort is projected to con-
tinue through the 2028–29 school year, followed by a two-year period of decline driven by lower birth
rates. If the capture rate does not increase, kindergarten enrollment is expected to stabilize at around
2,700, an approximately 30% decrease from the five-year pre-COVID average, due to demographic fac-
tors such as a smaller number of age-eligible children. Overall, elementary school enrollment (grades
K–5) is expected to decline from 18,695 to 18,427 students. The decline is projected to continue through
the 2037–38 school year, when enrollment will begin to recover from its low of 16,435.
Middle school (grades 6–8) also declines in the 2026–27 school year, from 9,753 to 9,590 (-1.7%).
As smaller elementary cohorts progress through the middle school grades, enrollment will continue to
decline to a low of just 7,624 students in 2040, interrupted only by a brief, two-year period of growth
in the 2033–34 and 2034–35 school years.

4.4
Forecast Accuracy and Uncertainty
Enrollment forecasts are utilized as a school planning tool and as a basis for community discussions
about future school facility needs. Forecasts are by their nature uncertain and rely on assumptions
that are likely to be violated to some extent. The most important sources of uncertainty in the current
district-level forecast are future trends in migration to/from the PPS district and the trajectory of capture
rates.
The last projection series was completed in the spring of 2025 using data through the fall of 2024.
These forecasts can be examined for their one-year enrollment forecast error, which was 184 students
or 0.4% lower than the final enrollment in fall 2025. The errors were concentrated in Kindergarten,
which was forecast for 112 students (3.8%) under final enrollment; ninth grade, which was forecast for
79 students (2.3%) under final enrollment; and first grade, which was forecast for 72 students (2.4%)
under final enrollment. Other grades were within 2% error, and the overall average error across all
grades (MAPE) was 1.37% (Table 4.3).
The methodology used in the current forecast is substantially similar to that used in previous PPS
enrollment forecasts produced by the Portland State University Population Research Center. There-
fore, another approach to assess potential forecast error is to compare actual enrollments with previous
forecasts from 2010 that used similar data and methodologies (Table 4.4).
The overall 1-year median forecast error since 2010 is 0.5%. In the early 2010s, forecasts failed
to anticipate the mid-2010s decline in enrollment. Before the pandemic, the middle-series forecasts
were consistently within 1% of the total of PPS K-12 in the first year, and the errors rarely exceeded
3% in the longer run. The COVID-19 pandemic negatively affected all accuracy metrics starting in
the 2020-21 school year. The enrollment declines resulted in a one-year middle-series forecast error of
