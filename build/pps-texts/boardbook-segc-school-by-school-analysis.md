Detailed Executive Summary of Findings  
Portland Public Schools, hereafter referred to as (PPS), implemented the Enrollment and 
Program Balancing initiative as part of a broader district effort to address enrollment imbalances, 
program access, and related equity concerns across Southeast Portland schools. As part of this 
effort, PPS partnered with and commissioned Dr. Terrance L. Green and his team to conduct an 
equity audit evaluation of the enrollment and program balancing initiative process and its 
implementation. The partnership officially began on January 20, 2026, and the final report was 
submitted on June 10, 2026 and the final school-by-school analysis was submitted on June 29, 
2026. . 
 
Many PPS stakeholders and participants commonly referred to this effort as SEGC, which stands 
for Southeast Guiding Coalition. Since SEGC was frequently used by participants during 
interviews and conversations to describe the broader process, we use SEGC in many places 
throughout this report to reflect participants’ language. Unless we are specifically referring to 
members of the Southeast Guiding Coalition itself, references to SEGC in this report should be 
understood as referring to the broader Enrollment and Program Balancing initiative and its 
related processes. 
 
The purpose of this evaluation is to provide PPS with empirical data to better understand how 
SEGC-related changes were experienced by students,2 families, principals, educators, and staff, 
in schools that experienced boundary changes, program relocations, enrollment shifts, and other 
related transitions. The report also examines patterns in student outcomes in these impacted 
schools before and after SEGC implementation, while recognizing that these outcomes are 
shaped by multiple factors and should not be interpreted as solely caused by SEGC. 
 
Findings from this evaluation are intended to identify areas of equity and inequity across the 
SEGC process, implementation, and outcomes, including how decisions were made, 
communicated, implemented, supported, and experienced over time. Through this report, we aim 
to provide useful, evidence-based, and humanizing data that can inform PPS’s future decisions 
about enrollment balancing, boundary changes, program relocation, school consolidation and 
closures, and other districtwide changes in ways that are more equitable, transparent, and 
responsive to school communities.

Introduction to School-by-School Findings  
This section presents school-by-school findings from the SEGC Equity Audit Evaluation, and to 
do so, we draw on both qualitative, quantitative and survey data. While our prior report 
summarized findings across all schools, this section examines how SEGC-related changes were 
experienced, implemented, and interpreted within individual school communities. This 
school-level analysis is important because SEGC did not land the same across all schools. In 
part, because each school entered the process with different histories, enrollment patterns, 
program identities, leadership conditions, staffing capacity, facility contexts, and relationships 
with families and communities. For that reason, the findings below are intended to show both the 
common themes that emerged within SEGC-impacted schools and the local conditions that 
shaped how each school absorbed, responded to, and made meaning of SEGC-related changes. 
 
Methodological Notes About Our Qualitative School-by-School Analysis  
First, the perspectives shared in the qualitative findings section reflect the experiences and 
viewpoints of the administrators, teachers, staff, and other participants who contributed to this 
study. These perspectives offer important insight into how SEGC-related shifts were experienced 
within particular school communities. However, they should not be interpreted as representing 
every perspective within a given school. Schools are complex communities, and individuals 
within the same school may have experienced these shifts in different ways. Consistent with 
qualitative research principles of thick description,4 5 the findings here are intended to elevate 
patterns and themes that emerged from participant accounts while providing sufficient context 
for readers to understand the conditions under which these perspectives were shared. As such, we 
honor the value of participant voices while also recognizing that additional perspectives may 
further deepen, extend, or nuance our understanding. 
 
Second, our preliminary analysis draws on in-depth qualitative case data collected from 9 of the 
20 schools (45%) impacted by SEGC changes. In this analysis, we treat each school as a 
qualitative profile or case. A case, in qualitative research, is a bounded setting or unit of analysis 
that allows researchers to examine experiences, processes, relationships, and conditions in 
context.6 Here, each school case helps illuminate how SEGC-related changes were understood 
and experienced within a particular school community. 
 
These schools were not intended to serve as a statistically representative sample of all affected 
schools. Rather, they were selected to provide deeper insight into varied school-level 
experiences. Therefore, as noted earlier, our approach is best understood as purposeful sampling

Analytic Note on Lived Experience, Observation, and Perception 
It was important for our team to distinguish between participants’ lived experiences and direct 
observations, on the one hand, and their broader perceptions or generalized interpretations, on 
the other. We aimed to make this distinction because both forms of evidence are important, but 
they do different analytic work. Lived experiences and direct observations help us understand 
what participants personally saw, did, heard, or experienced. For example, teaching in 
classrooms with more than 30 students, navigating student behavioral crises, losing a colleague, 
or supporting families through confusing transitions were treated as direct experiences. 
 
At the same time, participants often used those direct experiences to make sense of broader 
patterns, including their perceptions of the SEGC process, the district’s intentions, 
decision-making fairness, or which students, families, and schools were advantaged or harmed 
over time. These perceptions should not be dismissed, because they help explain how 
participants interpreted the meaning and consequences of what they experienced. 
 
However, differentiating between direct experience and broader interpretation is important 
because it allows us to be clear about the kind of evidence each claim represents. So, in this 
report, when we refer to what participants saw, experienced, or described directly, we treat those 
accounts as evidence of lived experience. When we refer to what participants felt, believed, or 
perceived about intent, cause, fairness, or systemwide impact, we treat those accounts as 
meaningful interpretations grounded in experience, but not as formal causal evidence on their 
own. 
 
This distinction helps preserve the integrity of our analysis. It also allows us to honor 
participants’ voices and the patterns that emerged across their accounts, while also being careful 
not to overstate what the data can establish. In this way, perceptions are not treated as “less real” 
than lived experiences, but rather, they are treated as important evidence of how people made 
sense of district decisions, trust, equity, and harm in the context of SEGC-related change. 
 
Influence of Context on Findings  
We treat participants’ accounts as important and credible evidence of how SEGC-related changes 
were experienced within school communities. Throughout the analysis, we also triangulated 
interview and focus group findings with available survey responses, administrative data, 
enrollment records, staffing records, and other district documents when those sources were 
available. At the same time, the context in which data were collected should be acknowledged. 
During parts of the data collection period, participants were navigating budget reductions, 
staffing cuts, declining enrollment, and rumors or concerns about possible school closures or

How to Read The School Profile Cases  
Each school case is organized around six guiding questions that align with the audit’s broader 
domains and research questions. For each school, we use these questions to examine how 
SEGC-related changes were experienced, interpreted, and implemented within that specific 
school context: 
1.​ To what extent did the SEGC process affect administrator, educator, and staff morale and 
retention, including among personnel of color where the data allowed us to examine this? 
2.​ To what extent did administrators, educators, and staff feel equipped to support students 
and families during and after SEGC-related changes? 
3.​ How did administrators, educators, and staff experience SEGC-related changes in terms 
of emotional well-being and professional support, including personnel of color where this 
information was available? 
4.​ How did students and families from historically underserved communities experience the 
quality of learning environments after SEGC-related shifts? 
5.​ How did administrators, teachers, and staff describe the quality of learning environments 
after SEGC-related shifts, particularly in relation to middle grades programming and 
supports? 
6.​ How did SEGC-related changes play out at each school, and what factors shaped how the 
school experienced, absorbed, and responded to those changes? 
 
For each case, we first summarize the intended SEGC-related changes for that school, based on 
district documents and available SEGC materials. This provides important context for 
understanding what changes were planned or formally adopted before we discuss how 
participants experienced, interpreted, and responded to those changes at the school level.

Anticipated SEGC Changes  
District-related SEGC documents identified four anticipated changes for Bridger: 
●​ a program relocation out of the school  
●​ a program relocation into the school  
●​ a change in instructional model 
●​ attendance boundary changes 
 
At the time of SEGC planning, Bridger was described as a co-located K–5 school with a small 
two-strand Spanish DLI program and a single-strand neighborhood program. The school had 337

Overview  
In this profile case, we share findings from survey data interviews and focus groups conducted 
with Bridger participants to better understand how SEGC-related changes were experienced 
within the school community. Since participants included individuals who were at Bridger 
before, during, and after SEGC implementation, their accounts offer insight into how the school 
changed over time and how those changes were understood by people working inside the 
building. Rather than treating SEGC as the sole cause of later conditions at Bridger, this chapter 
examines how participants connected SEGC-related shifts to broader patterns they experienced 
in morale, professional support, school climate, learning environments, student and family 
experiences, and the school’s capacity to absorb and respond to change. These accounts help 
illuminate how district-level decisions were lived, interpreted, and managed at the school level. 
 
Participants  
We conducted three focus groups and one individual interview with Bridger-based participants, 
which included nine school-based participants in total. Of these participants 6 of 9 (66%) of them 
were at either Bridger or Creative Science school prior to SEGC changes. Collectively, they 
spoke from the perspective of educators and staff who experienced SEGC-related changes in the 
daily life of Bridger, including changes connected to enrollment, programming, staffing, student 
needs, family communication, school culture, and transition support. Additionally, 15 Bridger 
staff responded to the staff survey and there were 87 Bridger respondents to the family and 
caregiver survey. Importantly, where the data did not clearly identify participants’ own racial 
identities or provide explicit information about personnel of color, we do not make claims about 
differential experiences among staff of color.

Findings  
 
Morale and Retention 
Participants described the SEGC-related changes as contributing to low morale, emotional 
exhaustion, and concerns about retention, layered on top of other system-wide stressors such as 
curriculum shifts, behavior challenges, and full time equivalency (FTE) reductions. Several 
teachers shared that during the first merged years, they regularly contemplated leaving the 
profession or taking extended leave, and they spoke of colleagues who had already taken leave 
and might not return. One participant described a year in which the combination of new 
grade-level expectations, class composition, and post-merger dynamics made continuing feel 
unsustainable, noting that they considered formal leave because “another year like that” felt 
untenable. Another participant described a year of teaching following the merger as one in which 
they “wanted to quit, like, every day,” underscoring the depth of strain experienced at the 
classroom level. 
 
Participants also linked morale to declining support and resource cuts over time. One participant 
explained that while the school initially received additional positions and support roles following 
SEGC, within a relatively short period those “gifts” were rolled back, with roughly a dozen or 
more FTE lost over two years. Classroom teachers echoed that they previously had a social 
worker and two counselors, but now saw neighborhood students receiving fewer services even as 
needs remained high. Altogether, these lived experiences fed a perception that staff were being 
asked to absorb major structural changes and higher needs with shrinking support, which, in 
participants’ view, contributed to the erosion of morale and increased the risk of burnout and 
attrition. 
 
The staffing record tracks the arc participants describe. Total staffing at Bridger stood at 46 FTE 
in 2020–21, declined to a low of about 33 FTE in 2022–23, then rose to 49 FTE in 2023–24 as 
the Creative Science team arrived (see Figure 1.1). It settled near 39 FTE in the two years that 
followed. The pattern participants summarize as an initial boost that was later rolled back is 
visible across these swings, and the scale of loss they describe, on the order of a dozen or more 
positions, is consistent with the size of the movements in the record. The leanest year, 2022–23, 
coincided with the conversion itself, when the building had the fewest adults available even as it 
was absorbing the most change.

Feeling Equipped to Support Students and Families During and After SEGC Changes 
Across the conversations, participants differentiated between their own efforts to support 
students and families and the systems and structures provided to help them do so. At the 
individual level, educators described extensive work to maintain communication, build 
relationships, and provide continuity for families, even as they themselves felt under-informed 
and overextended. For example, some teachers talked about regular newsletters, informal 
meet-and-greets, and early relationship-building so that the first real contact with families did not 
happen only at parent-teacher conferences. These were framed as deliberate choices to buffer 
students and families from the confusion and stress of the transition. 
 
At the same time, participants frequently emphasized that they did not feel systemically 
equipped. Teachers reported that information given to families about SEGC-related changes 
sometimes did not match what staff were told, or that messages arrived at different times or with 
different levels of detail, which made it hard to answer questions or provide consistent guidance. 
One participant characterized their experience of supporting families during and immediately 
after the SEGC transition as “flying blind,” explaining that each fall always brings a new group 
of families, but in this period there was no additional clarity or structure provided despite the 
scale of change. Another participant described the absence of deliberate planning for a smooth 
merger, such as joint community-building, clear protocols, and coordinated messaging, saying 
they did not see “deliberate, intentional” efforts to make the transition run well. 
 
Another participant similarly described walking into the merged context without transition plans 
or clear district guidance, recounting that it felt like the school had simply been handed “two 
buildings” with an expectation to make them function as one without a roadmap. Over time, 
participants perceived that promised supports, like dedicated community agents or mental health 
staff, were removed, which they felt further undermined their ability to respond to student and

Participants’ descriptions of their emotional well-being point to high levels of stress, anxiety, and 
emotional wear-and-tear, with SEGC-related changes acting as a significant contributing factor 
alongside other system pressures. Teachers talked about needing daily personal strategies, such 
as meditation, therapy, or medication, simply to sustain the work, even when they also 
emphasized that they cared deeply about students and often loved key aspects of teaching.

conditions. They pointed to large class sizes with many students who had significant academic, 
behavioral, or safety needs; complex new curriculum requirements; and insufficient staffing or 
training to meet those demands. This produced a recurring emotional theme, which was that 
educators’ sense that no matter how hard they worked, it was impossible to fully meet 
expectations, which contributed to feelings of inadequacy and dread about coming to work.

How SEGC-Related Changes Played Out at Bridger and Factors Shaping the Experience 
Across our Bridger-specific conversations, participants collectively described SEGC as 
reconfiguring Bridger’s identity, enrollment, and internal dynamics, with effects that were then 
amplified by leadership churn (three principals during a three year period), resource cuts, and 
district-level instability. Before SEGC, participants remembered Bridger as a Title I school with 
a mix of neighborhood and dual language immersion students, extensive cultural celebrations, 
and a strong sense of being a close-knit, collaborative community. The SEGC decisions moved 
dual language immersion, shifted Title I status, and brought in a focus-option community used to 
a high degree of autonomy and parent influence, which participants experienced as changing 
both the student body and the power dynamics within the school. 
 
Staff were generally very skeptical that the SEGC changes advanced equity. Among the staff 
who answered these survey questions, very few believed the changes were shared fairly across 
schools, and none believed the changes avoided creating new barriers for underserved students. 
Staff were also not convinced that SEGC made class sizes more equitable or balanced enrollment 
in meaningful ways. In short, staff survey responses suggest that many saw the changes as falling 
short of the equity goals PPS had attached to the process. Participants said:  
 
“Barriers were only created, and I don't even know how to accurately capture that here. 
Everyone is adjusting, but we're all worse for it.” 
 
“We were promised a classroom teacher for 3 years. That promise was broken and we have 
EXTREMELY high needs students and only 2 teachers instead of 3.” 
 
Participants perceived that the merger unfolded without a clear, shared vision or adequate 
planning. They described minimal structured opportunities for joint culture-building, limited 
clarity about how positions and roles would be allocated, and repeated changes in school-level 
leadership during the early years of the transition. Over time, they reported that after an initial 
“boost” in staffing, the school experienced substantial cuts that left larger classes and fewer 
adults to manage increased student needs and more complex programming expectations. One 
survey respondent commented:  
 
“It stressed all the teachers with the unknown and lack of leadership from principals from either 
schools. They made the merger harder than it needed to be and created more divide and lack of 
accountability and communication.” 
 
Participants also situated their experiences of SEGC within a broader context of trauma and 
instability. In addition to the merger, they referenced the COVID-19 period, the loss of a beloved 
colleague, and the deaths of two students as events that deeply affected the community and 
shaped how staff interpreted subsequent changes. These lived experiences fed perceptions that 
 

## Page 22

22 
the system did not adequately recognize or address compounding harms, and that the school was 
often left to manage large-scale change with insufficient guidance and repair. As some 
participants indicated:  
 
“We went from a close and connected community to being torn apart from our colleagues and 
community…and then trying to rebuild with less support.” 
 
“We’re trying to do everything that a comprehensive middle school can do without the FTE or 
resources available. It almost feels like the system is trying to kill mid-level.” 
 
The accounts above suggest that Bridger’s post-SEGC experience cannot be understood as the 
result of a single change or decision. Rather, participants described a layered and cumulative 
process in which program relocation, enrollment shifts, staffing reductions, leadership instability, 
reduced supports, and unresolved community tensions interacted over time. While participants 
continued to describe deep commitment to students, families, and the school’s educational 
vision, they also portrayed a school community that was asked to absorb significant change 
without the level of planning, communication, staffing, and emotional repair needed to make that 
change sustainable. For PPS, Bridger’s experience points to the importance of treating school 
reconfiguration not only as a technical enrollment or facilities matter only, but as an equitable, 
human, relational, and instructional transition that requires sustained support before, during, and 
well after implementation.

Clark Elementary School  
 
Anticipated Changes  
District-related SEGC documents identified four anticipated changes for Clark Elementary 
School (hereafter Clark):  
●​ a building use change 
●​ a program relocation out of the building 
●​ a new student and program assignment into the building 
●​ the reopening of Clark as a K to 5 elementary school connected to the Harrison Park 
pathway 
 
Prior to SEGC implementation, the Clark building housed Creative Science School, a K–8 focus 
option program with 419 students in 2021–22 and a 2025–26 forecast of 452 students. Under the 
SEGC recommendation, Creative Science would relocate from Clark to Bridger, and the Clark 
building would become the new K–5 location for Harrison Park elementary students, including 
students in the Mandarin DLI program. The expectation was that Clark would serve a newly 
configured K–5 school made up of Harrison Park neighborhood students and Mandarin 
elementary DLI students, with an estimated 349 students if the change had been in place in 
2021–22 and a 202–26 forecast of 372 students. The projected utilization for the new Clark K–5 
was 70.5% if it were in place in 2021–22 and 75.2% in the 2025–26 forecast. 
 
In short, Clark was expected to shift from housing a K–8 focus option program to serving as a 
neighborhood K–5 school connected to Harrison Park. The recommendation was intended to 
keep Harrison Park K–5 students together, maintain the Mandarin DLI program at the 
elementary level, and locate elementary students in a building aligned with their assigned middle 
school pathway. 
 
Overview  
In this profile, we share findings from our data collection with participants at Clark to better 
understand how SEGC-related changes were experienced within this school community. 
Participants included less than five individuals. Their accounts offer insight into how the 
reopening and reconfiguration of Clark as a neighborhood K–5 school was experienced by 
people working inside the building during and after the transition. Rather than treating SEGC as 
the sole cause of later conditions at Clark, we examine how participants connected SEGC-related 
shifts to broader patterns they experienced in morale, professional support, school climate, 
learning environments, student and family experiences, and the school’s capacity to absorb and 
respond to change. These accounts help illuminate how district-level decisions were lived, 
interpreted, and managed at the school level.

As such, the racial equity implication of this finding is that school reconfigurations and program 
changes can strengthen one form of equity while creating new forms of racialized displacement. 
Lent’s conversion to whole-school Spanish DLI appears to have created a stronger and more 
coherent Spanish DLI model and increased the share of Latino/a/x students at the school, which 
is a positive. However, the same change also coincided with a substantial decline in Black and 
Asian student enrollment and the movement of a more racially diverse group of neighborhood 
students to Marysville and other schools. This means that the equity question cannot only be 
about whether the school grew its Latino/a/x enrollment or whether the DLI model was 
educationally valuable. The more complex and nuanced question is whether PPS sufficiently 
accounted for how the school reconfiguration would affect Black students, Asian students, 
multiracial students, non-DLI neighborhood families, and the receiving schools expected to 
absorb students who no longer remained at Lent. In future program conversions, PPS should 
evaluate racial equity not only by who benefits from a strengthened program, but also by who is 
moved, who loses access, and whether receiving schools are resourced to support the students 
and families affected by that change. 
Moreover the school reconfiguration changed the district-retention pattern for this community as 
well. In the year before the conversion, the Lent group left PPS at 7%, below the district rate of 
9%. This was a population the district retained at above-average rates. In the conversion year, 
departures rose to 13%, nearly double the prior rate and above the district's 9%. The 
displacement turned a below-average-departure community into an above-average one. 
For the students who stayed at Lent, academic and behavioral outcomes (which we mentioned 
above) are positive, supporting participants’ sense that the full DLI model has been good for the

these results do not prove causality, they describe diverging outcomes for a cohort of students, 
some of whom stayed at Lent and others who left. 
Ultimately, the roughly 43% of students who stayed fared well in the reconfigured Spanish DLI 
school, most of whom were Latino/a/x or white. There were measurable costs for the 57% who 
were displaced, including longer commutes and bus rides, adjusting to new school climates and 
rules, and building new relationships with peers and adults, and these students were 
disproportionately Black and Asian Lent neighborhood families.  
Overall Conclusion About the Lent Case 
The Lent case, again, cannot be accurately described as either a simple success story or a simple 
story of harm. Instead, Lent illustrates one of the central tensions of SEGC, which is that a 
school reconfiguration can strengthen one equity goal while simultaneously creating new equity 
concerns. On one hand, participants described Lent’s current whole-school Spanish DLI model 
as more coherent, intentional, culturally affirming, and professionally sustaining for many 
Latino/a/x students, families, bilingual educators, and staff of color. Staff morale also appears to 
have improved over time, and students who remained at Lent showed positive academic, 
attendance, and behavioral patterns. 
 
At the same time, these gains were accompanied by significant costs. Lent’s conversion 
disrupted the school’s prior identity as a racially and linguistically diverse, walkable 
neighborhood school, produced substantial staff turnover, left some staff feeling under-informed 
and under-supported during implementation; and displaced a majority of the pre-conversion 
student group. The displacement was racially disruptive, with Black and Asian neighborhood 
students especially likely to leave Lent, while Marysville and other receiving schools absorbed a 
more racially diverse group of students who did not experience the same positive outcome 
patterns as students who remained at Lent. In sum, Lent demonstrates the promise of a strong, 
culturally affirming Spanish DLI model, but it also shows the racial equity risks of achieving that 
model through a process that displaced other students of color and altered the school’s 
neighborhood identity.

Overview  
Participants described Marysville as a racially, culturally, and linguistically diverse school 
community with long-standing relationships, deep staff commitment, and significant student and 
family needs. Rather than treating SEGC as the sole cause of later conditions at Marysville, this 
chapter examines how participants connected SEGC-related shifts to broader patterns they 
experienced in morale, retention, professional support, school climate, student and family 
experiences, learning environments, and the school’s capacity to absorb and respond to change. 
 
Findings  
 
Impact of SEGC-Related Changes on Marysville 
Participants described Marysville as a resilient, community centered school that had already 
navigated several major transitions before and around the SEGC period, including shifting from 
a K–8 to a K–5 configuration, rebuilding after a devastating fire, and adapting to changes in how 
students with social emotional and disability related needs were served. In participants’ accounts, 
SEGC did not arrive in isolation. Rather, it layered onto a school community that was already 
managing significant organizational change. 
 
Also, participants described the SEGC process and related district policy shifts as bringing an 
additional cluster of changes to Marysville. These included Marysville taking on a receiving 
school role for some Lent neighborhood students and staff connected to Lent’s conversion to a 
whole school Spanish DLI model, serving students from a broader set of neighborhood and 
pathway changes, transitioning toward a more neighborhood inclusive model for students with 
IEPs, and continuing to support a growing number of students experiencing poverty and housing 
instability, including students connected to a nearby shelter. In considering these changes, 
participants framed Marysville not simply as a school with a modest boundary adjustment, but as 
a school absorbing multiple layers of transition at once. 
 
Despite these mounting pressures, staff consistently described Marysville as a “good place to be” 
with strong relationships, a stable and long-serving principal, and a culture where people tend to 
stay once they arrive. At the same time, they emphasized that SEGC-related enrollment and 
program shifts intensified challenges that were already present, including very large class sizes, 
increasingly complex behavioral and academic needs, and frequent crises that left staff feeling 
overextended and emotionally drained. Participants often held two truths at once, that is, 
Marysville was a caring, joyful, and inclusive community that many were proud to work in, and 
it was also a place where burnout felt “imminent for everybody” under current conditions, 
according to one teacher.

Morale, Burnout, and Staff Retention 
Across roles, participants described Marysville’s staff as unusually committed, collaborative, and 
cohesive, often using the language of “community” and “family” to characterize their 
relationships. Teachers and paraeducators spoke of working closely across grades, supporting 
each other’s students, and building strong ties with colleagues over many years. One staff 
member noted that many educators had completed their student teaching at Marysville and then 
remained for a decade or more, reinforcing a sense of continuity and deep investment in the 
school. 
 
At the same time, this cohesion was often described as emerging in part from shared hardship. 
Several participants characterized the staff as “trauma-bonded,” referencing cumulative 
experiences of school fire and displacement, K–8 restructuring, SES program transition, 
COVID-19 disruptions, and then SEGC-related shifts layered on top. In this context, morale was 
described as both strong, in the sense of commitment and mutual care, and simultaneously 
fragile, in the sense that staff felt pushed to or beyond their sustainable limits. Participants 
shared:  
 
“I think part of that is like a trauma bond, you know, it’s a lot…we’re pretty tight.” 
 
“Marysville has always been a good place to be.”

the burnout and lack of support,” underscoring how these overlapping stressors contributed to 
attrition.

implement that vision effectively for students, families, and staff.

Quality of Learning Environments After SEGC-Related Shifts 
Participants described Marysville’s post-SEGC learning environment as both deeply caring and 
significantly strained. Again, educators consistently characterized the school as inclusive, 
relationship-rich, and committed to students. At the same time, they described large classes, wide 
skill ranges, intensive behavioral needs, and frequent disruptions as making it harder to provide 
the academic instruction, differentiation, and enrichment they wanted all students to receive. 
 
Teachers in early grades described classrooms of approximately 25 to 28 students, including 
multiple students with IEPs, students who are nonverbal, and students with developmental or 
safety needs that required significant environmental adaptation. Participants described removing 
materials that could be swallowed, limiting some enrichment activities because they were unsafe 
for particular students, and managing transitions with intense adult supervision.  
 
Upper-grade teachers described classes with more than 30 students, but with many students 
performing two or more grade levels below expectations, alongside students who were on grade 
level or advanced. Participants said this range made differentiation extremely difficult. One 
participant noted that students who were ready for deeper learning sometimes received less 
attention because staff had to prioritize crisis management and foundational skills. 
 
At the same time, participants described powerful examples of social inclusion that they 
associated with the inclusive model. They described classmates making room for a child who 
rarely joined carpet time, students helping nonverbal peers access preferred items, and entire 
classes “rallying around” students with significant needs. One participant reflected: 
 
“It was like the class just rallied around him and he was so loved and cared for… That’s the kind 
of thing that I think is what we need in the world. But we also somehow need to get them to show 
growth in MAP scores or whatever.” 
 
Another participant described the tradeoff this way: 
 
“I feel like it’s a slight on the academic side for a boost on the social aspect of it.” 
 
Participants therefore did not describe Marysville’s learning environment as simply weak or 
strong. Rather, they described a caring and inclusive environment where social-emotional 
growth, empathy, and peer inclusion were visible, but where academic instruction was harder to 
deliver at the desired level because of class size, student needs, staffing constraints, and frequent 
interruptions.

How SEGC-Related Changes Played Out at Marysville 
Participants described SEGC-related changes at Marysville as a convergence of boundary 
adjustments, enrollment shifts, program changes, grade reconfiguration, and the implementation 
of the inclusive neighborhood model. These changes occurred within the context of a Title I, 
shelter-serving school already supporting students and families with substantial needs. 
 
Marysville became the neighborhood school for a group of students reassigned from Lent. 
Participants estimated that roughly 90 to 100 students and about 11 staff members, including 
teachers and para-educators, transferred from Lent to Marysville in the initial year of the 
transition. Marysville’s catchment area also expanded from a compact, walkable zone to a much 
larger area stretching roughly from 72nd to 112th, crossing major roads and I-205. As a result, 
Marysville shifted from a school participants described as bus-free to one with multiple bus 
routes, including routes serving students from the shelter. 
 
Marysville also transitioned from a K–8 to a K–5 school as Kellogg reopened for middle grades. 
At the same time, the school phased out self-contained SES classrooms and became a fully 
inclusive neighborhood school, serving students with diverse IEP needs in general education 
settings unless they required alternative placements. Two pre-K classrooms were also added and 
initially integrated into Marysville’s staffing and support structures, though participants 
described those classrooms as later becoming more administratively separate while remaining on 
campus.

“There was a lot of really… upset and tears and anger… I’m fine with that. I’m not going to try

financial or political considerations often appeared to outweigh the district’s stated equity goals. 
 
Administrative data help clarify why participants experienced the 2022–23 to 2023–24 window 
as especially intense. During that period, Marysville moved through its pandemic enrollment 
trough, absorbed displaced families, experienced approximately 39% staff turnover, brought in 
roughly 37% new staff, lost 8.5 FTE, saw its exclusionary discipline rate rise from effectively 
zero before the changes to among the highest among SEGC schools, and added bus routes for a 
community that had previously been largely walkable. The clustering of these changes in a short 
period supports participants’ account that Marysville was absorbing multiple major transitions at 
once.

Participants 
In this chapter, we share findings from survey respondents interviews and focus groups 
conducted with school based participants at Lewis. Across the Lewis conversations, we heard 
from 13 unique school-based participants. The participant group provided a strong longitudinal 
perspective on the SEGC transition. Eleven of the 13 participants, or 84%, worked at Lewis 
before, during, and after SEGC-related changes. Their perspectives allowed us to compare how 
participants described Lewis’s pre-SEGC identity, staffing, enrollment, and school conditions 
with how they described the school after SEGC implementation. The remaining 2 participants 
were connected to Lewis during and after the SEGC-related changes, which offered insight into 
how those changes continued to shape the school’s current conditions even for staff who did not 
have the same pre-SEGC point of comparison. Eleven Lewis staff responded to the staff survey 
and there were 43 respondents to the family and caregiver survey.  
 
Overview  
In this chapter, we examine how participants connected SEGC related changes to broader 
patterns they experienced in morale, retention, professional support, school climate, student and 
family experiences, racial and socioeconomic diversity, and the school’s capacity to absorb and 
respond to change. These accounts help illuminate how district level decisions were lived, 
interpreted, and managed at the school level by the educators, staff, and administrators 
responsible for supporting students and families through transition. The goal of this profile is to 
illuminate how SEGC related decisions were experienced at Lewis not only as boundary or 
enrollment balancing changes, but as changes that affected the daily work of educators, the 
experiences of students and families, the school’s racial and socioeconomic composition, and the 
identity and functioning of the school community.  
Findings  
Participants Described Lewis as a High-Functioning School Community Strained by 
SEGC-Related Enrollment Loss

boundary changes that reduced enrollment, shifted the school’s racial and socioeconomic 
composition, contributed to staffing reductions, and disrupted some students’ middle school 
pathways.  
At the same time, participants consistently described Lewis as a high-functioning, tight-knit, and 
“special” school community whose staff culture, whole-child focus, and neighborhood 
relationships helped the school absorb some of the SEGC changes. One person summarized the 
school this way:

especially compared to other schools in the district, but layered with profound fatigue, grief over 
repeated cuts, and growing distrust of district leadership. One educator captured the dual reality: 
“I'd say, relatively speaking, compared to a lot of other schools…morale [here] 
probably higher than those. At the same time…budget goes down, expectations go 
up…I feel like we're like on this tightrope right now.”

When asked whether they felt safe raising concerns during SEGC-related changes, most

district leaders would listen or respond. Participants generally described Lewis as a place where

“I felt safe raising concerns to my principal, but at the same time I didn't feel like

“It's probably not safety, it's just apathy. It's like, well, why bother? I just don't have

How SEGC Played Out at Lewis and What Shaped That Experience 
Across the Lewis data, participants described SEGC as reshaping the school primarily through 
enrollment loss, boundary changes, staffing reductions, and disrupted student pathways. 
Participants consistently described Lewis as becoming smaller after SEGC-related boundary 
changes, with enrollment declining from roughly 400 or more students to approximately 260 
students. They connected this decline first to the loss of key boundary areas, including areas east 
of 52nd Avenue and, in earlier boundary changes, areas between 39th and 41st, and then to 
COVID-19-related shifts and broader demographic changes. Participants emphasized that, from 
their perspective, Lewis lost boundary area and enrollment without receiving an offsetting 
program, population, or resource addition in return. 
Participants also described significant staffing and program reductions as part of Lewis’s 
post-SEGC experience. These included the loss of multiple paraeducators, Learning Center 
paraeducators, reading specialists, partial reductions in PE and music, and substantial reductions 
in library and ELL staffing. Staff also anticipated the possibility of multilingual learner services 
dropping to zero FTE despite the continued presence of students needing multilingual learner 
support. In participants’ view, these reductions affected the school’s ability to provide the same 
level of individualized support, intervention, enrichment, and whole-child programming that had 
long been central to Lewis’s identity. 
Participants further described one-class cohorts and blended grades as important features of 
Lewis’s current conditions. Some cohorts now have only one classroom moving through the 
grade levels, and staff anticipated multiple 1–2 and 2–3 blended classrooms in the coming year. 
Participants viewed these structures as challenging because they may be misaligned with the 
adopted curriculum, developmentally difficult for students, and demanding for teachers who 
must plan for multiple grade levels within the same classroom. 
Overall, these are concrete school-level changes, including enrollment decline, boundary 
shrinkage, reduced FTE, fewer support positions, and changing class structures. Staff interpreted 
these changes as evidence that Lewis experienced SEGC largely as a loss of students, staff, and 
capacity rather than as a reconfiguration that brought new resources or opportunities. This does 
not mean SEGC alone caused every challenge Lewis now faces. However, the Lewis case shows 
how boundary changes, declining enrollment, staffing formulas, budget pressures, and broader 
demographic shifts can combine to make a strong school feel increasingly vulnerable.

Findings  
 
How SEGC Shaped Harrison Park 
SEGC-related changes at Harrison Park unfolded as part of a dense web of transitions. The 
school shifted from a K–8 model to a comprehensive 6–8 middle school, underwent major 
capital construction, absorbed new feeder patterns, received the middle-grades portion of a 
Mandarin DLI program, and navigated the broader context of the pandemic, labor actions, and 
budget instability. Participants emphasized that these changes did not land as isolated policy or 
technical adjustments. Together, they reshaped Harrison Park’s demographic profile, learning 
opportunities, school identity, and day-to-day functioning. 
The central story of Harrison Park is therefore one of expanded opportunity accompanied by 
significant disruption. Participants described meaningful improvements for middle school 
students, including a more developmentally appropriate grade configuration, more electives, 
greater access to specialist teachers, a stronger library experience, improved classrooms, air 
conditioning, and a rebuilt interior environment. At the same time, they described demographic 
shifts, equity concerns, limited district implementation guidance, and a sense that school-level 
staff had to absorb and solve many of the practical challenges created by SEGC. 
Administrative records support participants’ account that the reconfiguration year marked a clear 
shift in the school’s composition. During the K–8 years, Harrison Park’s enrollment was about 
81% students of color. After SEGC-related changes, that share declined to 72% in 2023–24 and 
67% in 2024–25. White enrollment rose from about 19% during the K–8 years to 33% in 
2024–25, Black enrollment declined from roughly 23% to 14%, and multilingual learner 
enrollment declined from about 36% to 23%. Participants experienced these shifts not only as 
demographic changes, but also as changes in the culture, identity, and equity context of the 
school.

Participants consistently described SEGC implementation at Harrison Park as a process in which 
school-level staff had to create systems, solve problems, and manage complex implementation 
tasks without a clear district playbook, defined responsibilities, or sufficient central-office 
guidance. Participants did not describe themselves as lacking commitment or capacity. Rather, 
they described having to rely heavily on their own experience, relationships, and initiative 
because the transition did not come with a clear implementation playbook. One participant 
described the challenge of staffing and planning the new middle school while simultaneously 
managing the departing elementary school: 
“I was in charge of doing the staffing and hiring and getting all the people ready for the middle 
school, and at the same time doing all of that work for the elementary school that was 
departing… There was not this, ‘Here’s this guidebook, here’s what you’re supposed to be 
doing.’ It was just I had to figure it out.” 
Participants also described how some SEGC implementation supports were not clearly defined. 
In one account, a person assigned to support planning arrived without clear direction from the 
district about what the role was supposed to involve. As the participant recalled, when asked, 
“What were you told to do?” the person responded that they had not been given specific 
guidance.

Learning Environments After SEGC 
Participants were clear that, from a programmatic and facilities standpoint, Harrison Park’s 
middle school students now experience a richer learning environment than they did under the 
K–8 model. Participants described the K–8 configuration as limiting the school’s ability to fully 
serve middle school students. One participant reflected: 
“We were not doing our middle school students justice when we were K–8… nine grade levels… 
a Mandarin immersion school… a district behavioral classroom… I could do all of those things 
marginally, but never felt like I was doing an outstanding job at any one thing.” 
As a comprehensive middle school, Harrison Park was able to offer more electives, arts and 
music opportunities, library access, and interventions. One participant described the shift this 
way: 
“Our middle school students definitely have a richer experience… they didn’t have a whole lot of 
electives… now they have access to librarian, band, art, more often.” 
Family survey responses align with this programmatic improvement. Sixty-four percent of 
current Harrison Park families agreed that their student could access the classes, programs, or 
learning opportunities they needed, and 74% agreed that their student had access to programs 
and opportunities regardless of race, language, disability, or family background. Participants also 
described the capital project as a major improvement to the learning environment. One 
participant emphasized that the rebuilt interior signaled care and respect for students: 
“This school now has been rebuilt from the inside. The outside facade is the same, but 40 million 
has been put into this building… every student deserves to be in a space that is aesthetically 
pleasing where they feel valued.”

school environment: 
“What’s happening here… it’s pretty minimal. It’s a pretty chill environment in terms of 
behaviors…We don't have very many fights. We don’t have very many big problems… we have a

Overall Interpretation 
The Harrison Park case shows why SEGC cannot be evaluated only by whether a school gained 
or lost enrollment, received a program, or changed grade configurations. According to 
participants, Harrison Park experienced real gains, including a stronger middle school structure, 
improved facilities, more electives, better access to specialist teachers, and a clearer 
developmental focus for early adolescents. Participants consistently recognized these benefits 
and, in many cases, viewed the shift to a comprehensive middle school as educationally 
important. 
At the same time, these gains came alongside substantial disruption and unresolved equity 
concerns. Harrison Park became less racially and socioeconomically diverse than it had been as a 
K–8 school, lost a substantial portion of its Black and multilingual learner population, and 
entered a new phase in which families’ experiences of diversity differed depending on whether 
they were comparing Harrison Park to its former K–8 identity or to the schools and 
neighborhoods from which they arrived. Participants also described a district process that felt 
reactive, overly responsive to the loudest voices, and insufficiently guided by a coherent 
equity-centered implementation strategy. 
What helped Harrison Park absorb the change was not simply the design of SEGC. It was the 
school’s internal stability. Long-standing leadership, strong staff cohesion, shared hardship, and 
deep commitment to students helped the school manage a concentration of transitions that might 
otherwise have produced greater instability. The personnel record supports this interpretation: 
staff continuity through the immediate conversion was unusually strong, even though more 
recent retention patterns suggest that the strain may be catching up over time.

Findings 
Overall Impacts of SEGC-Related Changes at Kellogg 
Across the Kellogg conversations and survey respondents, participants described SEGC-related 
changes as part of a larger set of structural shifts that shaped the school after its reopening. 
Kellogg was described as a diverse middle school with a substantial Spanish DLI program, a 
strong internal commitment to students, and a staff culture that participants viewed as improving 
over time. At the same time, participants emphasized that the consolidation of Spanish DLI, 
boundary adjustments, and post-reopening growth occurred alongside deep budget cuts, staffing 
reductions, and ongoing uncertainty about the school’s capacity to sustain the support students 
needed. 
Additionally, participants described SEGC as strengthening the coherence and identity of the 
Spanish DLI pathway, while broader fiscal and staffing conditions limited the school’s ability to 
fully realize the benefits of that change. In their accounts, Kellogg’s staff remained committed to 
students and to one another, but the school was repeatedly asked to do more with fewer adults, 
fewer support roles, and less stability. 
Participants described Kellogg as a place where students want to be and where families actively 
seek access. One participant pointed to the school’s strong demand for transfers and its “unique 
diversity,” including Latino/a/x, Black, Asian American, Native, Pacific Islander, multiracial, 
and white students. Administrative records are consistent with this portrait of Kellogg as a 
substantively diverse middle school. Latino/a/x students increased from 30% of enrollment in 
2021–22 to 34% in 2024–25. Black students remained between 5% and 6%, multiracial students 
remained at about 10%, and Asian students declined from 11% to 7%. White students remained 
the largest single racial group, at approximately 41% to 42% across all four years. In other 
words, Kellogg’s diversity was real and stable through the SEGC implementation window, rather 
than simply a new outcome produced by SEGC. 
Participants also emphasized that Kellogg’s diversity and program strengths unfolded within a 
difficult staffing context. They described the school as losing 11 staff members in one year and 
five more the next, including key support roles and experienced DLI teachers. Participants did 
not always separate SEGC-related impacts from broader budget and staffing challenges. 
However, they consistently described the same pattern: SEGC created or reinforced important 
programmatic possibilities at Kellogg, but the school’s ability to deliver on those possibilities 
was constrained by staffing cuts, increased workloads, and fragile student support systems.

Participants described morale at Kellogg as both improved and fragile. On one hand, participants 
described a stronger internal culture, better relationships with current building leadership, and a 
more cohesive staff climate than in earlier years. One participant described staff survey results as

internal culture amid continued structural instability.

Quality of Learning Environments 
Participants described Kellogg’s learning environment as shaped by the tension between a 
standardized comprehensive middle school model and the diverse needs of the students the 
school serves. Participants noted that PPS uses high-quality instructional materials aligned to 
state and national standards, which can provide consistency across schools and smoother 
transitions for students who move between buildings. However, a participant also described 
limits to this approach: 
“The curriculum doesn’t always speak to the diverse needs of our kids… teaching and learning

of model.” 
One person argued that Kellogg needed more differentiated, inquiry-driven, and experiential 
approaches to better engage its students. However, implementing that kind of pedagogy at scale 
was difficult under current staffing and resource constraints, especially when many students 
entered below grade level in reading and math. Other teachers’ accounts reinforced this concern. 
They described large classes, multiple preps, and wide-ranging academic needs as barriers to 
deeper instruction. One teacher framed the challenge this way: 
“Teachers can only go so deep when you’re doing three different things with 35 different kids. So 
how can we get more adults… working with smaller groups of kids?”

reading and math interventions as transformative for some students, especially when students’ 
behavioral challenges were connected to academic frustration or low skill levels. Participants 
described students in intervention classes as feeling “seen and heard and helped and met where 
they are.” They also described notable academic growth among some participating students.

How SEGC Changes Played Out at Kellogg 
At Kellogg, SEGC-related changes were experienced most directly through the consolidation of 
the Spanish DLI program and related boundary and feeder-pattern adjustments. Spanish DLI 
students from Mt. Tabor arrived in 2023–24, two years after Kellogg’s reopening. Administrative 
records show 58 students leaving Mt. Tabor’s Spanish DLI program and 62 students being added 
to Kellogg’s enrollment. One teacher described moving from another middle school to Kellogg 
along with students when DLI was centralized. The participant acknowledged that the transition 
was disruptive for students, some of whom experienced being “tossed together” and continued to 
describe the move as “mildly traumatic.” Over time, however, this participant viewed the 
consolidation as programmatically beneficial: 
“Specifically here, having the dual language community all centered in one location is much 
more helpful… It is better for the teachers and it’s better for the program at the middle school 
level, in my opinion.” 
This consolidation did not occur in isolation. Participants located it within a broader sequence of 
events. Kellogg’s reopening, the multi-year SEGC process, leadership transitions, the end of 
pandemic relief funds, declining state and district budget capacity, and repeated staffing 
reductions. During 2023–24 and 2024–25, several pressures converged: Spanish DLI 
consolidation, principal transition, major FTE reductions, and an exclusionary discipline rate that 
more than doubled between 2022–23 and 2024–25. These patterns do not prove causation, but 
they show how SEGC-related changes interacted with other conditions that shaped Kellogg’s 
experience.

how Kellogg absorbed these changes. They contrasted earlier leadership instability and strained 
teacher-administration relationships with the current principal’s more relational, people-centered

underfunding, staffing reductions, leadership transitions, and reduced support capacity limited 
the school’s ability to realize the full promise of those changes for students, families, and staff. 
Overall Interpretation 
The Kellogg case illustrates one of the central tensions of SEGC which is that a structural change 
can be programmatically sensible and still be difficult to implement equitably if the school does 
not have stable staffing, adequate support roles, and sufficient resources. Kellogg appears to have 
benefited from the consolidation of Spanish DLI in several important ways. Participants 
described a stronger DLI identity, more collaboration among DLI teachers, and a clearer middle 
school pathway for students. Administrative data show the program grew substantially after 
consolidation. 
At the same time, Kellogg’s ability to sustain these gains was undermined by repeated staffing 
reductions, ongoing turnover, reduced support capacity, large classes, increased discipline rates, 
and persistent absenteeism. Participants described a school with strong commitment, improving 
internal culture, and promising interventions, but also one where staff were stretched thin and 
where key student supports remained fragile.

A Strong School Community Experiencing Cumulative Loss 
Across the Mt. Tabor administrative data, survey response, and conversations, participants 
described SEGC-related changes as a multi-year process that reshaped the school’s size, 
composition, staffing, and program capacity. Participants repeatedly emphasized that Mt. Tabor 
entered SEGC as a large, over-capacity middle school with multiple feeders and language 
pathways. After SEGC-related changes, the school lost a feeder, portions of another boundary 
area, and its Spanish DLI program. Participants connected these changes to a substantial 
enrollment decline, repeated staffing reductions, larger classes, fewer electives, reduced supports, 
and diminished racial, linguistic, and socioeconomic diversity. 
 
Administrative records support the broad arc participants described, while clarifying some of the 
details. Mt. Tabor’s enrollment declined from 680 students in 2020–21 to 473 students in 
2024–25, with the steepest single-year drop occurring between 2022–23 and 2023–24, the year 
the Spanish DLI program left. Staffing also declined substantially, from about 58 FTE in 
2020–21 to about 33 FTE in 2025–26. The largest headcount drop corresponded with the 
departure of the Spanish DLI program. 
 
 
Participants did not describe this as a single moment of disruption, but as a slow erosion. Each 
year brought new reductions, sometimes several staff positions at a time, until Mt. Tabor fell 
below the enrollment level participants understood as necessary to sustain a robust 
comprehensive middle school. Several participants described this as especially frustrating 
because SEGC had been presented as part of a broader regional balancing strategy, but the 
anticipated future changes that might have replenished Mt. Tabor’s enrollment did not occur. 
 
Nevertheless, nearly every participant emphasized Mt. Tabor’s strong internal culture. 
Participants described the school as caring, collaborative, well-organized, and deeply committed 
to students. They praised the strength of staff relationships, the consistency of schoolwide 
systems, and the sense that students experience Mt. Tabor as a safe and supportive place to learn. 
As one participant explained: 
 
“This is such a good school because we have great, seasoned, caring teachers who 
collaborate…I wish more kids could come to the school, because they get a good education.” 
 
Another participant described the school’s climate this way: 
 
“We have a really inclusive learning environment at the school…We work really hard to promote 
a safe, positive school climate where kids can feel like they belong.”

Participants described Mt. Tabor staff as a committed to supporting students and families, but not 
consistently equipped with the information, resources, or staffing needed to manage 
SEGC-related changes well. Some district support was available, including communication

participants emphasized that much of the practical work of helping families understand and 
navigate the changes fell to individual schools. One participant explained: 
 
“More of it… was on individual schools to be responsive to their own communities and figure

resources needed to respond to student and family questions, none agreed that they had support 
to address challenges, and only 1 of 10 agreed (10%) that they had the support needed overall.

“It’s hard because it’s just me… I have students that I know their needs are not getting met, even

How SEGC Played Out at Mt. Tabor 
Participants described SEGC’s impact at Mt. Tabor as a sequence of interconnected changes. 
Before SEGC, Mt. Tabor served a neighborhood program, a Japanese dual-language immersion 
pathway, and a Spanish DLI pathway. The school’s combined enrollment placed it above 
capacity and allowed it to sustain a broad range of programming and supports. 
 
SEGC changed this structure by removing a major feeder, relocating the Spanish DLI middle 
grades program to Kellogg, shifting portions of another boundary area, and leaving the Japanese 
DLI at Mt. Tabor. Over time, these decisions contributed to a steep enrollment decline, which 
then triggered staffing reductions and program contraction. Participants described losing 
specialized roles, intervention supports, restorative justice capacity, climate positions, and 
elective sections. 
 
Participants also emphasized unrealized balancing moves. They believed SEGC was not 
supposed to stand alone, but was expected to be followed by future boundary work in other parts 
of the district that would bring new enrollment to Mt. Tabor. When that did not happen, 
participants experienced the school as being left under-enrolled and under-supported. As noted 
by a participant:  
 
“[SEGC] was not supposed to live on its own…we figured within that three-year time span… 
another movement in North and Northeast… would happen, and we would see an influx… That 
did not materialize at all.” 
 
Another participant summarized the outcome: 
 
“The biggest change is a lot fewer students, which has resulted in less staff, less diversity… Our 
school is under-enrolled and has suffered great losses while we’re waiting for the changes to 
come.” 
 
Administrative data confirm that 2023–24 was an especially intense year of change. The Spanish 
DLI program departed, enrollment dropped sharply, Latino/a/x and multilingual learner shares 
fell, the multiracial share rose, exclusionary discipline incidents per student increased, and total 
FTE continued its decline. These patterns should not be interpreted as proof that SEGC caused

why participants experienced SEGC as a major turning point in the school’s trajectory. 
 
Several conditions shaped how Mt. Tabor absorbed these changes. Leadership continuity and 
communication helped buffer some of the disruption. Staff collaboration and strong internal 
norms also helped the school adapt to new schedules, enrollment realities, and student needs. 
Meanwhile, participants described district politics, inequitable community voice, and delayed 
follow-up planning as intensifying the harm. They perceived that certain “pockets of privilege” 
had greater influence during SEGC, while less advantaged communities struggled to have 
concerns heard. Participants also described the long duration of the process and virtual 
engagement during the pandemic as barriers to authentic participation. 
 
Facility and accessibility constraints also shaped the experience, especially for students and staff 
with disabilities. Participants described an older building where physical access issues made 
inclusion more difficult and where district commitments to inclusion were not always matched 
by accessible infrastructure. 
 
Overall Interpretation 
The Mt. Tabor case illustrates how a school can remain strong internally while being weakened 
structurally. Participants described Mt. Tabor as a caring, collaborative, and academically strong 
middle school. Survey responses support this view, particularly around school climate, 
collaboration, and high-quality learning environments. Yet the same participants and survey 
respondents also described low system-level trust, unmanageable workload, insufficient 
resources, large class sizes, and deep frustration with SEGC’s implementation and 
follow-through. 
 
The strongest throughline across the Mt. Tabor data is cumulative loss. Participants experienced 
SEGC as reducing the school’s enrollment, staffing, diversity, language programming, family 
engagement structures, and comprehensive middle school capacity. Administrative records 
support much of this account, especially the enrollment decline, Spanish DLI departure, staffing 
contraction, and loss of Latino/a/x and multilingual learner enrollment. At the same time, the 
records complicate some perceptions, including the idea that the school became proportionally 
whiter overall. This distinction matters: participants were describing real losses in specific 
students, communities, programs, and relationships, even when some schoolwide percentages 
moved in more complex directions. 
 
In short, SEGC at Mt. Tabor was experienced less as a single boundary adjustment and more as a 
redistribution of students, programs, and resources that left the school smaller and less 
comprehensive than it had been. Mt. Tabor’s internal culture helped the school continue serving

students well, but it also masked how much the school was absorbing. For PPS, the key lesson is 
that strong school culture cannot substitute for adequate enrollment, staffing, accessibility, 
program support, and district follow-through. Future districtwide changes should not assume that 
a high-functioning school can absorb repeated losses without weakening the very conditions that 
made it strong.

Kelly’s student composition also shifted somewhat during this period. White enrollment declined 
from approximately 55% to 45%, Latino/a/x enrollment increased from approximately 17% to 
23%, and multilingual learner enrollment remained high, ranging from roughly 43% to 51%. 
According to participants, these changes appear to reflect broader neighborhood and enrollment 
patterns rather than a direct SEGC effect, but they provide important context for understanding 
the school community participants described. 
Participants consistently portrayed Kelly as calm, relationally strong, and deeply connected to 
students and families. They described staff as “banding together” through repeated stressors and 
credited long-standing relationships, school-based leadership, and student support systems with 
helping the school remain stable for families. The overall picture is of a school whose internal 
culture helped buffer some of the most visible impacts of district-level uncertainty, even while 
participants remained concerned about future cuts, program changes, and consolidation. 
Morale and Retention 
Participants described staff morale at Kelly as resilient but pressured. On one hand, they 
described deep attachment to the school and strong relationships among staff. One participant 
explained: 
“I work with a lot of the same people that were here when I was hired, and it feels like family… 
It’s kind of a tough school… and that consistency for the families that we work with… I feel 
really proud of.” 
Another participant described staff relationships as a kind of “trauma bonding,”20 emphasizing 
that repeated stressors had pushed staff to rely on one another: 
“I feel like we're very bonded. It's almost like trauma bonding, like we have to band together, 
because every year… people are getting unassigned. There’s staff reductions, there’s fewer

Emotional Well-Being and Professional Support 
Participants located SEGC within a broader period of exhaustion that included COVID-19 
recovery, budget crises, contract disputes, layoffs, furlough discussions, and anxiety about future 
consolidation. One participant described the district context as “really grim times financially.” 
Another described the emotional dissonance of being asked to focus on compliance metrics 
immediately after major staffing reductions. One person noted that:  
“The district just got done un-assigning nearly 250 people… then they called all the principals

dehumanizing, it's transactional.  
Participants also connected emotional well-being to class size and student need. Some described 
recent years with smaller class sizes as far more sustainable and emotionally healthy. One 
participant explained: 
“This year has been, like, amazing… I had 18 kids [previously] and this year I had 19… Those 
are very small classes, and that has highly affected my emotional well-being.”

staff respondents agreed that their current workload was manageable. Family survey comments 
also reflected concern about class size, with one family writing that “30 kids is too many” and 
another identifying “large class size and difficult behavior issues” as negative factors affecting

Participants repeatedly emphasized Kelly’s importance for immigrant, refugee, multilingual, and

who rely on school-based relationships, language access, counseling, social work, food and

How SEGC-Related Changes Played Out at Kelly 
Participants did not describe Kelly as experiencing a major program relocation or grade 
reconfiguration directly through SEGC. Instead, again, as mentioned prior, they described 
SEGC-related and SEGC-adjacent dynamics as part of a broader pattern of district 
decision-making that affected the school’s sense of security. These included boundary and 
enrollment shifts, proposed 4/5 grade blends, the contraction of the Russian DLI program, 
transportation barriers, and concerns that Kelly could become part of a future consolidation 
process. 
The proposed 4/5 blend was one of the clearest examples participants raised. Participants 
described the cohort as having significant needs, including refugee students and students with 
serious trauma histories, and said school staff worked hard to prevent a blend they believed 
would undermine students’ ability to have their needs met. One participant explained: 
“There was a lot of concern about blending the fourth and fifth grade… that particular cohort 
had four or five students that had to be separated… several different administrators have had 
different values, [but] every single one identified this as a serious area of concern and has done 
as much as they could to prevent that from impacting our school or our students’ ability to have 
their needs met.” 
Participants also linked SEGC to future consolidation conversations. One participant described 
the school’s strategy as trying to secure a “seat at the table” rather than waiting for decisions to 
be made elsewhere: 
“It’s either going to happen without us or it’s going to happen with us. And if we don’t have a 
seat at the table, things will happen to us.” 
Participants described both risk and possibility in this future-facing conversation. The feared 
scenario was that the Russian DLI program could be removed, leading to a sudden loss of 
students, staff, and community identity. The more hopeful scenario was that Kelly could become

Overall Interpretation 
The Kelly case shows how SEGC-related change can be experienced indirectly, through 
uncertainty, program vulnerability, staffing concerns, and future consolidation risk rather than 
through one major immediate reconfiguration. Participants did not portray Kelly as a school that 
had been dramatically disrupted by SEGC in the same way as some other schools. Instead, they 
described Kelly as a strong and stable school community operating in a fragile district 
environment. 
The central tension is that Kelly appears to be working well for students and families, especially 
immigrant, refugee, multilingual, and low-income families, but participants worried that the 
conditions making the school work could be weakened by staffing cuts, transportation barriers, 
program contraction, grade-level blends, or future consolidation decisions. Administrative and 
survey data generally support this picture that enrollment remained stable, midyear enrollment 
growth was plausible, school climate and learning environment ratings were strong, and staff 
retention recovered after a difficult year. At the same time, workload, class size, and district 
communication remained areas of concern.

Conclusion: Lessons Learned from School-by School 
Analysis  
The school-by-school findings show that SEGC was experienced less as a single enrollment 
balancing initiative and more as a series of locally lived changes that landed differently across 
school communities. SEGC as a district-level change strengthened some program pathways, 
reduced crowding in some places, and created more developmentally appropriate grade 
configurations. However, at the same time, it also produced enrollment loss, staffing reductions, 
disrupted relationships, transportation burdens, diminished trust, and new equity concerns in 
other places. For this reason, SEGC should not be evaluated only by whether it balanced 
enrollment, improved school utilization rates, or relocated programs according to the district’s 
original plan. It should also be evaluated by how those changes were experienced inside schools 
by the students, families, educators, staff, and school leaders who carried the consequences over 
time. 
The central lesson from this school-by-school analysis is that racial equity and social justice are 
not achieved through intent or rhetoric alone. They become real, or can be often undermined, in 
implementation. Racial equity and social justice are experienced in whether families understand 
what is happening and why, whether students remain connected to peers, trusted adults, and 
accessible programs, whether receiving schools have the staffing and support to absorb change, 
whether historically underserved and racially minoritized communities carry disproportionate 
burdens, and whether the district follows through on what it promised. Across the schools, 
participants often recognized the stated equity goals behind SEGC, but they also described gaps 
between those goals and the lived realities of implementation.  
As such, these school-by-school findings offer several lessons for PPS.  
One major lesson is that strong schools can still be weakened by repeated losses. Several schools 
in our analysis were described as caring, collaborative, relationship-rich communities with strong 
internal cultures. These strengths helped schools absorb SEGC-related changes, but they did not 
eliminate the consequences of enrollment decline, staffing reductions, program movement, or 
reduced support. In some cases, the very schools that were able to “make it work” did so because 
staff and leaders stretched themselves to compensate for gaps in district planning and resources. 
Given this, PPS should be careful not to mistake school-level resilience for evidence that SEGC 
change was adequately supported or accomplished. A school’s culture, relationships, and trust 
are real assets, but they are not unlimited resources. 
A second lesson is that program coherence can come with displacement costs. Some SEGC 
decisions appear to have strengthened important programmatic goals, especially around dual 
language immersion and middle school configuration. For example, consolidating language

pathways or creating fuller middle school structures may provide real benefits for students who 
remain in or enter those programs. However, those benefits must be weighed alongside the 
experiences of students and families who were moved, who lost neighborhood access, who faced 
longer transportation routes, or who entered receiving schools under strained conditions. Future 
program decisions should therefore ask not only whether a program is stronger after 
reconfiguration, but also who was displaced to make that strength possible and whether those 
students and families were equitably supported. 
A third lesson is that receiving schools need more than enrollment. They need organizational 
capacity to equitably and properly support students. Several schools were asked to receive 
students, staff, programs, or new grade configurations while also managing existing student 
needs, budget pressures, staffing instability, and COVID-19 post-pandemic recovery. Receiving 
students is not simply a matter of assigning them to a new building. It requires transition staffing, 
transportation planning, multilingual communication, student support services, family 
engagement, special education and multilingual learner capacity, and time for school 
communities to build trust. Future districtwide enrollment changes should include a 
receiving-school readiness plan before implementation begins. 
A fourth lesson is that families and staff judge equity by follow-through, not just words. Across 
school communities, participants often distinguished between being invited to speak about SEGC 
changes and believing their input mattered. Many described SEGC engagement processes where 
they shared concerns, attended meetings, testified, reviewed maps, or supported families, but did 
not see clear evidence that community input shaped final decisions or implementation supports. 
This distinction matters for trust. Future family and community engagement processes related to 
enrollment should make clear what is open for influence, what has already been decided, what 
criteria will guide decisions, and how community feedback changes the final plan. Without 
visible follow-through, engagement can be experienced as procedural rather than meaningful. 
A fifth lesson is that PPS should evaluate the cumulative burden before adding new changes to 
any school community (we offered this lesson in our phase 1 report that included our 
cross-school findings). Many SEGC-related changes were not experienced one at a time. They 
overlapped with leadership transitions, construction, budget reductions, labor disruptions, 
COVID-19 recovery, staffing cuts, program changes, and broader demographic shifts. We 
learned that a boundary change that looks manageable on paper may feel very different in a 
school that has already lost staff, absorbed displaced students, changed grade configurations, or 
endured repeated uncertainty. Future enrollment planning should include a cumulative impact 
review that asks what a school has already absorbed, what supports remain in place, and what 
additional burden a proposed change would create. 
Finally, PPS should build stronger mechanisms for post-implementation learning and course 
correction measures. School change does not end when boundaries are redrawn, programs are

relocated, or students move buildings. For students, families, and staff, the real work begins after 
implementation. Future districtwide enrollment changes should include public checkpoints, clear 
indicators of success and harm, family and staff feedback loops, and the authority to adjust 
course when unintended consequences emerge. These mechanisms should track not only 
enrollment and utilization, but also staffing stability, class size, student belonging, transportation 
burdens, program access, discipline, attendance, family trust, and whether receiving schools are 
able to provide the support students need. 
In sum, the school-by-school findings suggest that future enrollment balancing, program 
relocation, consolidation, closure, or rightsizing efforts should be designed as multi-year equity 
implementation processes, not one-time technical and policy decisions. PPS should be able to 
show not only what will change, but why it is necessary, who will benefit, who may be burdened, 
what supports will be provided, how promises will be tracked, and how the district will respond 
if the lived experience of students, families, and staff does not match the intended goals. The 
most important lesson from SEGC is that districtwide change must be judged not only by the 
logic of the plan, but by the justice of the experience.
